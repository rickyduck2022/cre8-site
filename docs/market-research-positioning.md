# cre8 positioning: market research

**Prepared:** 11 November 2026  
**Scope:** UK-first validation of the proposed community, Creator Pro and curated Sample Pack offers  
**Status:** Desk research and positioning hypotheses; not a market-size study, pricing approval or forecast  
**Related:** [Business plan](./business-plan-uk.md), [monetization strategy](./monetization-strategy.md)  
**PDF edition:** [Download PDF](./market-research-positioning.pdf).

## Executive summary

cre8's strongest differentiator is not "creator tools at £9/month." It is the
combination of a focused underground-music community, pack/crate organization,
and granular, revocable access to unreleased music. The closest access-control
tools are oriented toward labels and promotion teams; mainstream creator
platforms are adding monetization and fan-gating, but the reviewed offerings do
not foreground named-recipient, dubplate-style sharing as a free community
trust feature.

That is a plausible position, not demonstrated product-market fit. Producers
already use free chat and cloud-storage tools, and genre forums and music
communities have struggled to retain users or find sustainable revenue.
Creator Pro's proposed workflow features also compete in a crowded low-cost
tools market. Validate willingness to pay for a repeated release workflow
problem—especially access tracking and recipient management—rather than
storage, generic analytics or the cultural idea of exclusivity alone.

| Positioning hypothesis | Research read | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Producers need a better home for scattered releases and recipients | Plausible; free Discord, chat and file links are strong substitutes | Demonstrate a concrete workflow improvement over their current stack |
| cre8's release workflow plus trusted niche community is distinct | Potentially distinct; community retention is the highest strategic risk | Start with a narrow, active cohort and a use case that brings members back |
| A-List/private access should remain free | Strong differentiation from paid promo tools and fan paywalls | Preserve this trust boundary; monetize creator workflow value instead |
| £9/month Pro and 15% pack commission are viable | Unproven; both sit in contested pricing territory | Treat as experiments; ask for paid commitments and test repeat use |

## What is being tested

The current plan proposes free discovery, listening and ordinary community
participation; Creator Pro at £9/month for a delivered bundle of creator
workflow features; an optional £5/month supporter tier; and, later, curated
rights-cleared Sample Packs with a proposed 15% commission. The product
positioning emphasizes release management and trust rather than storage,
guaranteed promotion or rights cre8 does not own.

This report assesses the market evidence for that positioning. It does not
validate proposed features that are not yet shipped, legal readiness, music
rights, seller economics or final prices. See the [monetization strategy](./monetization-strategy.md)
and [UK licensing and payments checklist](./uk-licensing-and-payments.md) for
those decision gates.

### Competitive conclusion

The whitespace is a *combination*, not any single feature: trusted niche
community + organized releases/packs + explicitly granted and revocable
pre-release listening. Private promo platforms monetize campaign sending;
professional music-sharing tools sell secure links/catalog management; broad
creator platforms compete on reach, distribution and sales. The reviewed
examples do not establish that no competitor can serve the job—only that this
specific bundle is not their primary positioning today.

SoundCloud is the competitor to watch most closely. Its moves toward 100%
distribution royalty retention, direct-to-fan sales and fan-exclusive releases
make "all your artist tools in one place" a weak moat. cre8 should avoid
positioning itself as a smaller SoundCloud. Its more defensible claim is
creator-controlled access to unreleased music embedded in a trusted, scene-level
relationship graph.

## Demand-side evidence

### Evidence supporting the hypothesis

- **Exclusivity has cultural and commercial relevance.** UK dubplate-cutting
  services advertise cuts from around £25 for DJs, sound systems and producers,
  including unreleased tracks and edits. This supports willingness to pay for
  physical exclusivity, but does not directly show willingness to pay for
  software access controls. [Diz Lathe Cuts](https://dizlathecuts.co.uk/dubplate-cutting-uk)
- **Creator subscriptions can scale.** Music Business Worldwide reported Splice
  at 600,000 paying subscribers and over US$100 million in annual revenue in
  2025, alongside its acquisition of Spitfire Audio. This is evidence for
  spending on sample/production subscriptions at scale, not a forecast for a
  small community product. [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/splice-acquires-spitfire-audio-for-a-reported-50m-as-it-bets-on-ai-music-creation/)
- **The broader electronic music economy is growing.** IMS/MIDiA valued the
  global electronic music industry at US$15.1 billion in 2025, up 7% year over
  year. UK Music reported £8 billion GVA for the entire UK music industry in
  2024. Neither figure estimates cre8's addressable niche or software demand.
  [IMS/MIDiA](https://www.internationalmusicsummit.com/news/ims-electronic-music-business-report-2025-26) ·
  [UK Music](https://www.ukmusic.org/news/new-report-reveals-uk-music-industry-contributes-record-8-billion-to-uk-economy/)
- **Adjacent paid tools show that access workflows have value for some
  organizations.** Promo and private-sharing products sell recipient controls,
  campaign tracking and analytics. Their label/agency buyer base is materially
  different from cre8's independent-creator target.
- **There is a direct job-to-be-done analogue.** The Dubplate Player app turns
  cloud folders into a private streaming library for unreleased music. It is
  positioned as no-subscription/no-ads and does not provide cre8's described
  community layer—both validation of the use case and a warning that utility
  alone may not justify recurring payment. [Dubplate Player](https://dubplateplayer.com/)

### Evidence against / material risks

- **Free substitutes are already familiar.** Chat, Discord and cloud links solve
  basic file sharing, discovery and conversation. The value gap must be
  demonstrably better access control, recipient-level tracking, organization or
  repeat discovery—not merely another upload destination.
- **Niche communities are hard to sustain.** Public examples include the closure
  of Ampled and Nina Protocol and the shutdown or migration of genre forums such
  as Drum&BassArena Forum and Dubstepforum. These cases are not a statistical
  failure rate, and their models differ from cre8, but they make retention and
  operating sustainability a first-order risk. [Nina wind-down coverage](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/soundcloud-acquires-nina-protocol-the-independent-music-platform-winding-down-after-five-years/) ·
  [Drum&BassArena forum closure](https://breakbeat.co.uk/news/30365)
- **Creator Pro is in a crowded price band.** At around £9/month, it overlaps
  sample, distribution, mastering and secure-sharing subscriptions. Without a
  frequently used workflow or clear avoided cost, it risks becoming one more
  subscription to cancel.
- **Sample packs are not automatically a growth engine.** Generic packs face
  extensive substitutes and discovery competition. Curated products tied to a
  creator's sound and audience may fare better, but this needs buyer interviews
  and real purchase tests. Published "sample pack market size" estimates vary
  widely and often lack transparent methods; they are excluded from the case
  for cre8.
- **The 100-user launch base is too small for reliable conversion benchmarks.**
  Applying a broad 2–8% freemium range from generic SaaS benchmark summaries
  mechanically gives only 2–8 payers, but those rates are not a niche-music
  forecast. One or two users materially change the apparent percentage. Use
  actual paid commitments, repeat feature use and renewal evidence instead.
  [Example benchmark summary](https://userpilot.com/blog/freemium-to-premium/)

## Positioning and monetization implications

### Sharpen the lead

Lead with the trust and release-access job; describe the community and packs as
the context that makes the job useful. For example:

> **Share unreleased music with the people you choose. Keep your dubs, packs and
> DJ community organized in one trusted place.**

This is a research direction, not approved marketing copy. Avoid saying "leak
proof," promising confidentiality beyond the technical controls, implying
exclusive rights, or claiming the product replaces every cloud drive or promo
platform.

### Keep the business model sequence narrow

1. Keep ordinary community participation and access controls free, as proposed.
   This is a credible trust differentiator and supports creator-to-listener
   network value.
2. Test the £9 Pro price against a *specific delivered workflow*—for example,
   access history/export, repeatable release setup or bulk recipient
   management—rather than a list of future features. Do not charge for
   scheduling, branding or analytics until they work end to end.
3. Keep the optional supporter offer distinct from Pro and from A-List approval.
   Ask whether members actually want to support cre8; do not treat it as proof
   of creator-tool demand.
4. Before a pack marketplace, test a few rights-cleared products and interviews
   with both buyers and sellers. The proposed 15% is comparable to Bandcamp's
   standard digital share, but it is not validated for sample packs and may
   draw comparison to commission-free beat storefronts. Explain the service
   and costs behind any fee.
5. Treat SoundCloud as an ongoing strategic risk. Recheck its creator tools and
   access model before each pricing or positioning review.

## Recommended validation questions and decision signals

Interview active creators and regular DJs/listeners separately. Ask for the
last real release-share workflow, where it broke down, who needed access, what
they paid for alternatives, and what they would stop using. Then show a working
or accurately labelled prototype of recipient grants, revocation and access
history; avoid leading with a feature wishlist.

Prioritize these questions:

- Do creators repeatedly need to grant/revoke access to named recipients, or is
  this occasional and already handled adequately with private links?
- Is knowing who listened/downloaded worth paying for, and what decision does
  that information enable?
- Which Pro workflow saves enough repeated time or coordination to justify a
  monthly fee? Would creators pay at £7, £9 or £12 for that delivered bundle?
- Will DJs return to discover permitted releases and maintain crates, or do
  they prefer existing social channels and storefronts?
- Would a buyer purchase a particular curated pack at its real price and
  licence terms, rather than merely join an interest list?

Use the existing plan's small-pilot gates as learning thresholds, not statistical
proof: request explicit commitments at the shown price, observe activation and
feature use, and measure renewals, support time, contribution margin and
access/billing incidents over multiple cycles. Track eligible active creators
separately from total registrations. If creators will not renew, improve the
repeated workflow or stop the offer; do not manufacture conversion by
restricting ordinary community use.

## Sources, confidence and limits

Research snapshot: 11 November 2026. Competitor prices and product features can
change; official pricing pages are preferred where available, while some
comparisons and 2025–26 feature announcements rely on trade press or secondary
pricing snapshots. Treat the position as a desk-research hypothesis to verify
with users, not a complete competitor census.

Higher-confidence evidence includes published official pricing/feature pages,
the IMS/MIDiA report, UK Music's industry report and direct current UK
dubplate-service listings. Medium-confidence evidence includes trade reporting
of company scale and platform launches. Lower-confidence evidence includes
generic SaaS conversion/churn benchmarks, informal community listings, and
market-report vendors' sample-pack TAM figures; those are not used as proof of
cre8 demand.

No genre-specific UK revenue estimate for dub, jungle, UK garage or dubstep was
identified. Broad music-industry totals therefore provide context only. This
research also does not determine rights clearance, VAT, payment regulation or
consumer-law requirements; see the [UK licensing and payments checklist](./uk-licensing-and-payments.md).
